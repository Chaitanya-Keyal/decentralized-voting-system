% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

\projectname{} set out to show that an institutional election can run without
anyone having to trust the organiser with the ballot box or the count, and
that ballot secrecy can be added without giving that up. Both tiers were built
and run end to end. In a wallet election (tier 2), the voter list, the ballots
and the count are on a public chain, and the authority that created the
election cannot change any of them. An anonymous election (tier 3) keeps all of
that and hides who cast which ballot: the voter proves in zero knowledge that
they are on the published list, a nullifier stops them voting twice, and a
relayer pays the gas, so the voter needs no wallet and no cryptocurrency. A
complete anonymous election has run on Ethereum Sepolia in real time, with
voters registering, a relayer submitting their ballots, voting closed and the
result finalized by calls that anyone may make, and an audit in the browser
recounting it from the chain and IPFS alone.

The measurements support the design's main claims, with one cost that was not
expected:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anonymity has a fixed price.} Under mainnet's current rules a
        tier~3 ballot costs 296,692 gas against 80,221 for a tier~2 ballot.
        The difference, about 216,000 gas, is mostly the contract's check of
        the proof.
  \item \textbf{The price does not grow with the electorate.} From 4 to 65,536
        voters, a tier~3 ballot costs between 296,656 and 296,692 gas, and
        publishing a voter list costs 147,319 gas at every size. The work that
        does grow moves to the voter's device: proving takes 0.57\,s at two
        voters and 1.52\,s at 65,536 on one ARM64 core, but rebuilding the
        tree from the published list grows with every voter, to 15.8\,s at
        65,536. For a university electorate of tens of thousands, that rebuild,
        not the proof, is the slow step in the booth.
  \item \textbf{Voting is fast enough.} \note{B1: replace with the median and
        95th percentile from bench/results/b1-sepolia-*.csv.} In the first
        Sepolia samples a ballot was counted 8.7 and 11.2 seconds after the
        voter pressed Cast, about one block.
  \item \textbf{The testnet changed the costs.} Sepolia's Glamsterdam rules
        price new state several times higher than mainnet's. Creating an
        election there cost \note{3.09} million gas against 0.64 million locally. Under
        the same rule, relaying through an ERC-2771 forwarder cost about
        \note{127,000} gas more per ballot than relaying directly, which is why the
        relayer now submits ballots itself.
\end{itemize}

The security properties in Section~6 were tested by trying to break them:
double voting, forged and tampered proofs, and an authority acting beyond its
role all fail in the contract tests. Section~6 also sets out why chain data
alone cannot link a ballot to a voter (D3). \note{The attempt to do so on a real
election's data is still to be run; report its outcome here.} The results also have limits, and these
matter as much as the results. The registrar decides who is eligible, and nothing
on-chain can check that it registered only real voters, or each of them once.
The relayer sees when each ballot arrives and from which network address, so
anonymity against the operator depends on keeping the relayer and the
registrar apart, or on voters submitting from their own wallets. A voter can
still prove to a buyer how they voted, and anyone holding their key can vote
in their place. The running count is public while voting is open. The proofs
rest on Semaphore's trusted setup ceremony. Finally, everything here ran on a
testnet: the gas figures are exact, but what a ballot would cost in money on
mainnet depends on gas prices this project does not measure.

Within those limits, the project shows that a small institution can run an
election whose count nobody has to take on trust, with secret ballots, and with
voters who have never owned cryptocurrency. What it does not yet show is that
real voters, rather than simulated ones, can do this unaided; the usability
test with real participants is what would settle that.
