\subsection{Actors and stakeholders}

Six kinds of people or services take part in an election.
Table~\ref{tab:actors} summarises what each one can do and what the system
prevents them from doing, as implemented. The paragraphs after it explain why.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Actors, what the system lets them do, and what it prevents.}
  \label{tab:actors}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{2.8cm}L{6.2cm}L{6.4cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Actor} & \textbf{Can do} & \textbf{Cannot do} \\
    \midrule
    Election Authority
      & Create an election with its candidates, dates, ballot type and counting
        rule; change details before registration opens and candidates before
        voting opens; cancel before voting opens
      & Cast or change a ballot; change the voter list; stop voting once the list
        is published and its window has begun; withhold the result            \\
    Registrar
      & Check eligibility off-chain, register wallets or commitments, sign
        receipts, publish the voter list, its root and the voter count
      & Change the list once published; learn from the chain how anyone voted in
        an anonymous election; cast a ballot for a voter                     \\
    Voter
      & Register, cast one ballot, check that it was counted, audit the election
      & Vote twice; vote without being on the list                           \\
    Candidate
      & Be listed on the ballot, publish a manifesto
      & Anything privileged                                                  \\
    Auditor / Observer
      & Open registration and voting when due, close voting, publish the result;
        recount the tally, re-verify every proof, rebuild the root from the list
      & Change any ballot, the list or the count                             \\
    Relayer
      & Pay gas for anonymous ballots; see the choice in each; refuse to forward one
      & Forge, change or replay a ballot; learn who cast it from the ballot   \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Election Authority.}
The authority sets up the election but cannot end it on its own terms. Every
phase change after creation can be made by anyone once its time has come, so an
authority that dislikes how things are going cannot stall. What only the
authority can do happens before the first ballot and is recorded on-chain: it
can change an election's details until registration is due to open, its
candidates until voting is due to open, and cancel it until the published list
reaches its voting window. None of these lets it touch a ballot.

\paragraph{Registrar.}
Evidence of eligibility, such as a student's email address, must never end up
on a public blockchain. The registrar therefore checks it off-chain, either
through Google sign-in limited to the institute's domain or by emailing a
six-digit code to an institute address, and publishes only the voter list's
root, the voter count and the list's IPFS address, its content identifier
(CID), which names the file by a hash of its contents. Every registration
earns a receipt the registrar signs, over the wallet in tier 2 or the identity
commitment in tier 3. The list is published only after registration closes and
before voting is due to open, and it can never change, because a registrar that
could add entries during voting could also create ballots. In an anonymous
election, voters build their proofs from that public list, so the registrar
plays no part in voting itself.

\paragraph{Voter.}
In an anonymous election the voter's secret is created in their own browser,
and only the commitment is sent to the registrar; the browser offers the secret
as a backup file and can restore it on another device. A voter also needs to be
able to check that their ballot was counted, otherwise they are simply trusting
a contract instead of a person; the booth confirms a ballot only once the chain
records its nullifier. The downside is that a ballot a voter can prove is also
one they could be pressured into proving.

\paragraph{Candidate.}
Candidates get no special powers, because any special power would affect the
result they are competing for. They do not need any either: the candidate list
and the IPFS addresses of their manifestos are on-chain, so a swapped candidate
would be visible, and the tally is public.

\paragraph{Auditor and observer.}
Anyone can audit an election without signing up or asking permission, because
the ballots, the tally, the root and the voter count are all public. An audit
that went through our backend would only check what we say, not what is on the
blockchain, so the audit in the web application reads only the chain and
IPFS. It still passes with our API, indexer and relayer unreachable, as long
as the voter list can be fetched from IPFS.

\paragraph{Relayer.}
The relayer exists so that voters do not need cryptocurrency: it pays the gas
for anonymous ballots. It cannot change a ballot, because the choice and the
nullifier are bound into the proof, and it submits exactly the call the booth
built. It is the only one of our services that handles ballots during voting,
and any wallet can stand in for it. Because it can read each choice and decide whether to forward it, it also
opens up an attack, described in Section~\ref{sec:trust}.

\subsection{Functional requirements}

Table~\ref{tab:fr} restates what the system must do, as agreed in
Evaluation~1, with a third column recording how the implementation meets each
requirement.

\begin{longtable}{@{}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash\small}p{1.0cm}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash\small}p{7.0cm}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash\small}p{9.3cm}@{}}
  \caption{Functional requirements from Evaluation~1, and how each is met.}\label{tab:fr}\\
  \toprule
  \textbf{ID} & \textbf{The system shall} & \textbf{As built} \\
  \midrule
  \endhead
  \bottomrule
  \endlastfoot
  FR1 & Let the Authority create an election with its candidates, registration and voting dates, and a ballot description, and allow changes to the candidates only before voting opens. & Met. A factory deploys each election as a minimal proxy clone~\cite{eip1167}; the description and manifestos go to IPFS. The ballot type and counting rule are also chosen at creation and never change. \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  FR2 & Let the Registrar check eligibility off-chain, accept each commitment without putting personal data on-chain, and give the voter a signed receipt for it. & Met. Google sign-in limited to the institute's domain, or an emailed code, then a receipt signed by the registrar's key over the commitment (tier 3) or the wallet (tier 2), as EIP-712 typed data~\cite{eip712}, which the voter can check against the registrar the contract names. Email addresses stay in the registrar's database. \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  FR3 & When registration closes, have the Registrar publish the list of commitments and record its IPFS address, its root and the voter count in a single transaction, after which none of them can change. & Met. One call records all three, only between the close of registration and the start of voting; a second call reverts. The registrar publishes on its own once registration has closed. \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  FR4 & Move between phases only through an explicit, one-way transaction that fails if it is attempted before its scheduled time. Only the Authority may configure or cancel an election, and only before voting opens; anyone may close voting and publish the result. & Met, extended. Opening registration, opening voting, closing voting and finalizing are open to anyone once due. Cancellation is refused only once a published list has reached its voting window, so an election whose list never appeared, or whose voting window passed without voting opening, can still be cancelled rather than stay stuck. \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  FR5 & Accept a ballot only if it carries a valid zero-knowledge proof that the voter is on the published list, together with a nullifier and the chosen candidate. Record every nullifier, reject repeats, and reject any ballot beyond the voter count. & Met for tier 3, with the choice generalised to a bitmask under approval voting. The contract fixes the tree depth from the voter count and the proof's scope to its own address, so neither comes from the caller. Tier 2 applies the same rules with a Merkle proof and the voter's address in place of the nullifier. \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  FR6 & Have the booth download the published list, confirm that the Voter's commitment is on it, and build the proof locally, without asking the Registrar for anything at vote time. & Met. The booth rebuilds the tree, refuses a list that does not give the on-chain root and count, and proves in a Web Worker with proving files checked against pinned SHA-256 hashes. \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  FR7 & Let the Relayer check a ballot's proof, and that its nullifier is unused, before paying the gas, and return a signed receipt. Warn the Voter and offer direct submission if no transaction appears by a deadline, and let a Voter who has gas skip the Relayer entirely. & Met. Before signing a receipt the relayer also checks the election, and simulates the exact \texttt{castVote} call. The booth waits up to five minutes for the chain to record the nullifier. It warns when voting closes within five minutes, and always offers submission from any wallet. \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  FR8 & Change the tally only in the transaction that accepts a ballot, make the result permanent once it is published, and keep every ballot, the tally, the root, the voter count and the result readable from the blockchain alone. & Met. Each accepted ballot emits an event with its key and choice; the in-browser audit recounts from those events and compares with the contract's tally and winners. \\
\end{longtable}

\subsection{Non-functional requirements}

Table~\ref{tab:nfr} lists the qualities the system must have, unchanged from
Evaluation~1 except where noted, and the evaluation parameter that tests each.
Section~6 reports the measured values.

\begin{longtable}{@{}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash\small}p{1.5cm}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash\small}p{10.4cm}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash\small}p{5.4cm}@{}}
  \caption{Non-functional requirements and the parameter that tests each one.}\label{tab:nfr}\\
  \toprule
  \textbf{ID} & \textbf{Requirement} & \textbf{Tested by} \\
  \midrule

  \endlastfoot
  NFR1 & \emph{Ballot secrecy} (tier 3). Nothing on the blockchain links a ballot to a voter's commitment or registration. The choice itself is public; what stays hidden is who made it. Nothing on-chain links a ballot to a commitment; how well that hides a voter depends on the size of the list, the timing of ballots and the result.   \addlinespace[2pt]

  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR3 & \emph{One ballot per voter.} A second ballot from the same secret is rejected, and the number of accepted ballots never exceeds the published voter count. & D1, with fuzz tests \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR4 & \emph{Verifiability.} A Voter can confirm that they are on the published list and that their ballot was counted, and anyone can rebuild the root and recount the tally from the blockchain and IPFS, without our API, indexer or relayer. & D4, with our API and relayer unreachable \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR5 & \emph{Integrity of the count.} Only an accepted ballot changes the tally, and nobody, including the Authority, can change a tally, stop the count or block the result. & D5 \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR6 & \emph{Cost.} Every on-chain operation is measured in gas, and we report how much extra gas anonymity adds to a ballot. Gas is reported under both mainnet's current rules and Sepolia's Glamsterdam rules, since they differ several-fold. & A1 \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR7 & \emph{Latency.} Casting a ballot should feel like any ordinary blockchain transaction: the wait should come mostly from the blockchain itself rather than from our software, and proving should work on an ordinary phone. & B1 and B2 \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR8 & \emph{Scalability.} The gas cost of publishing the list and of casting a tier 3 ballot should not grow with the number of registered voters. A tier 2 ballot's cost grows slowly with the list, and the total cost of an election grows with the number of ballots cast. & C1 and C2, with C3 for the load limit \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR9 & \emph{No cryptocurrency needed} (tier 3). A Voter can register and vote with no wallet at all. & E2 \\
  \addlinespace[2pt]
  NFR10 & \emph{Usable without training.} A Voter with no experience of cryptocurrency can vote without help. & E1 and E3 \\
\end{longtable}

Of these, NFR2, NFR3 and NFR8 matter most. Ineligible or duplicate ballots would
make the result meaningless, and NFR8 is the claim the architecture rests on.

\subsection{Changes during implementation}\label{sec:req-changes}

Building the system changed some requirements and left one promise of
Evaluation~1 partly unmet. Each change and its reason are recorded below.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Approval voting was added.} Evaluation~1 assumed one candidate
        per ballot. An approval ballot, a non-empty set of candidates encoded
        as a bitmask, costs almost nothing extra to verify and suits committee
        elections, where several seats are filled at once. The rule is fixed
        when the election is created, so it cannot be changed once the
        authority sees how voters are leaning.

  \item \textbf{Both tiers became user-facing.} Evaluation~1 planned tier 2 as
        a stepping stone to tier 3. Tier 2 is kept as a choice for open votes,
        where it is about 3.7 times cheaper per ballot under mainnet's rules and
        needs no proving. Each tier has its own factory, and each election's
        contract reports its tier, so neither can be passed off as the other.

  \item \textbf{The counting rule lives in the election contract.}
        Evaluation~1 proposed a separately deployed counting library. The
        rules are instead an internal library compiled into the election
        contract. This meets the same goal: the code is public and verified
        before any election exists, and no election can switch to other code.

  \item \textbf{The relayer is its own process.} It has its own key, its own
        queue store and no access to the registrar's database, and it keeps no
        request logs. Keeping the two apart is the operator condition NFR1
        depends on (Section~\ref{sec:trust}).

  \item \textbf{The relayer checks more than planned.} Besides the proof
        and the nullifier (FR7), the relayer checks that the election is an
        anonymous \projectname{} election and simulates the call it will send
        before signing a receipt. A ballot the contract would reject
        therefore never earns a receipt, and the voter is told why. One queued
        ballot per nullifier, and a rate limit per client, protect the
        relayer's funds.

  \item \textbf{The relayer does not use the ERC-2771 forwarder.} Evaluation~1
        planned to relay ballots through a forwarder, each signed by a fresh
        key. The ballot's proof already binds the choice and the election, and
        \texttt{castVote} never reads the sender, so the signature added
        nothing the relayer could not already do or undo. It did add cost: on
        Sepolia a forwarded ballot used 521,094 gas against 393,900 sent
        directly, mostly for a new storage slot holding each fresh key's nonce.
        The relayer now calls \texttt{castVote} itself. The forwarder is still
        deployed and tested, for relayers that expect it.

  \item \textbf{Direct submission is open to any wallet.} Evaluation~1 allowed
        a voter with gas to skip the relayer. Since an anonymous ballot carries
        its own proof, any wallet can submit it, including a friend's. The
        booth says plainly that the submitting wallet is shown as having sent a
        ballot.

  \item \textbf{A registration stays with its first wallet or identity.} An
        email address registers one wallet (tier 2) or one identity (tier 3),
        and the pair never changes; registering again with the same one only
        renews the receipt. If a registration could move, a registrar that
        dropped or swapped a voter's entry could claim the voter had moved it,
        and the receipt would prove nothing. A voter who loses their tier 3
        secret before the list is published therefore cannot replace it, which
        is why the booth makes the backup file at the moment the secret is
        created.

  \item \textbf{No server-side proving.} Evaluation~1 kept server-side proving
        as a fallback in case phones could not prove in reasonable time.
        Proving in the browser worked, so the fallback was not built, and the
        voter's secret never leaves their device. Section~6 reports proving
        times by device: on a laptop, under a second for any list up to 4,096
        voters. \note{A phone's figure is still to come.}

  \item \textbf{Deployment had to follow the network's gas rules.} Sepolia
        adopted Glamsterdam on 6 October 2026~\cite{glamsterdam}, after
        Evaluation~1. Its EIP-8037~\cite{eip8037} prices new state at 1,530 gas
        per byte, which the local toolchain does not model, so the first
        deployment attempt set its gas limits from a local simulation and ran
        out of gas. Deployment now takes its gas limits from the network, and
        NFR6 reports both rule sets.
\end{itemize}

One promise of Evaluation~1 is not yet met. The usability test behind NFR10,
parameter E3, needs participants unfamiliar with cryptocurrency;
\note{state whether it was run, and with how many participants}. Where a requirement is met
only conditionally, as NFR1 is against our own operator, the condition is
stated in Section~\ref{sec:trust} rather than counted as met.

\subsection{Trust model and explicit non-goals}\label{sec:trust}

The proof system Semaphore uses, Groth16~\cite{groth16}, needs a one-time
trusted setup for each circuit. We do not run our own. The booth uses the
setup's published proving files for the tree depths 1 to 20, version 4.13.0,
and each file is pinned by its SHA-256 hash. The hashes were checked against
the npm package that distributes the same files, whose download matched the
registry's integrity hash. The booth refuses any file that does not match. The
contract tests then verify proofs made with these files against Semaphore's
on-chain verifier, which ties the proving keys to the deployed verification
keys. If every participant in the setup had colluded, they could forge proofs
that look valid. The contract's cap on the number of ballots (FR5) does not
depend on the setup, so it still holds in that case.

With that assumption in place, \projectname{} guarantees five things,
corresponding to NFR1 to NFR5. The first holds for anonymous elections only;
the other four hold in both tiers.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Ballot secrecy.} The proof shows only that the voter is on the
        list, and nothing on-chain links a ballot to a commitment. A voter is
        hidden among everyone else on the list and no further: in a small
        list, a ballot cast in a quiet hour, or a unanimous result, says more.
        Against our own operator secrecy needs one more condition. The
        registrar knows who registered, and the relayer sees when each ballot
        arrives and from which network address; run by the same people, the
        two could be matched. So the relayer must be run apart from the
        registrar, or the voter must submit through a wallet not linked to
        them.
  \item \emph{Eligibility.} Only someone on the published list can produce a
        ballot that the contract accepts.
  \item \emph{One voter, one ballot.} Each nullifier, or in tier 2 each
        wallet, can vote only once, and the number of ballots is capped at the
        voter count.
  \item \emph{Verifiability.} A voter can confirm that they are on the list
        and that their ballot was counted, and anyone can recount the tally
        from public blockchain data and IPFS.
  \item \emph{Integrity of the count.} Only an accepted ballot changes the
        tally, and anyone can close voting and publish the result, so no
        operator can change it or hold it back.
\end{itemize}

There are also eight things \projectname{} does not guarantee.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Coercion resistance and vote selling.} Anyone who holds a voter's
        secret, or its backup file, can cast that voter's ballot, and the voter
        cannot undo it. A buyer can check on-chain that they got what they paid
        for, remotely and at scale. Paper does better here, because a coerced
        voter can simply lie about how they voted.
  \item \emph{Fake voters.} A dishonest registrar could register people who do
        not exist. Publishing the voter count does not reveal this, because
        registration is voluntary and nobody else knows the true number. What
        it does achieve is fixing that number in public before any ballot is
        cast, after which the contract refuses any extra ballots. A registrar
        could still register fake voters early and use them late for whichever
        candidate needs the votes.
  \item \emph{Correctness of the voter list.} Nothing forces the published list
        to match who actually registered. A registrar could drop or swap an
        entry, and the list would still match the published root. The signed
        receipt (FR2) and the booth's check that the voter is on the list (FR6)
        make this provable, but only before voting opens, and neither one makes
        the registrar honest.
  \item \emph{Privacy from the relayer, and selective censorship.} The relayer
        sees each ballot's choice and the network address it came from, which
        is why it must be run apart from the registrar (above). It could also
        quietly drop the ballots for one candidate, and low turnout would hide
        it. The
        receipt, the booth's wait for the chain and the option to submit from
        any wallet (FR7) limit this, but direct submission needs someone with
        gas.
  \item \emph{Network-level anonymity.} The relayer or the RPC provider sees the
        network address a ballot comes from. The relayer keeps no request logs,
        but that is a promise rather than a guarantee. A real fix would need
        Tor or a mixnet.
  \item \emph{Secrecy of the running tally.} Ballots are counted as they
        arrive, so a partial result is visible during voting and could
        influence people who have not voted yet. The timestamps also help
        anyone who knows when a particular person voted to narrow down their
        ballot.
  \item \emph{Recovering a lost secret.} A voter who loses their secret and
        its backup cannot vote, because their registration cannot move to a
        new secret and no entry can be added once the list is published.
  \item \emph{Integrity of the served pages.} The voting key is made and kept
        by the web app in the voter's browser. Whoever serves that app could
        serve a copy that sends the key elsewhere, and a voter cannot easily
        tell. The source is public and anyone can build and serve their own
        copy, but a voter who uses ours trusts it, as they would trust any
        web wallet.
\end{itemize}

Section~7 takes up five of them: coercion, the relayer's view and its power
to censor, the running tally, lost secrets and the served pages. The other
three remain open. Fake voters and the correctness of the list come down to
trusting the registrar, and network-level anonymity needs tools outside this
project.
