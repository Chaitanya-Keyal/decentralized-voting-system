\section{Introduction}
% Source material: Evaluation 1 Section 1, revised to the system as built.
% ===========================================================================

Most institutional elections, from a student senate to a club committee, run on
a database that one organiser controls. Voters have to trust that organiser
with the voter list, the ballot box, the count and the records, and nobody
outside has any way to check that they were handled honestly.

\projectname{} is a voting application that removes the need for that trust in
the ballot box and the count. The voter list, the election schedule and the
count are held by smart contracts on a public test blockchain, Ethereum
Sepolia~\cite{sepolia}. The Election Authority that runs an election cannot cast
or change a ballot, cannot change the voter list once it has been published,
and cannot stop the count: once voting has ended, anyone can close it and
publish the result.

\paragraph{What was built.}
\projectname{} supports two kinds of election, chosen when the election is
created, and two counting rules, plurality and approval. Both kinds share the
same lifecycle, factory and voter list design.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Wallet ballots (tier 2).} A voter registers a wallet. When
        registration closes, the registrar publishes the list of registered
        wallets on IPFS~\cite{ipfs} and records its Merkle root~\cite{merkle},
        the number of voters and the list's IPFS address on-chain in one
        transaction. A ballot is sent from the registered wallet with a Merkle
        proof that the wallet is on the list. Everyone can see which wallet
        voted for whom, and the voter pays a little gas.
  \item \textbf{Anonymous ballots (tier 3).} The voter's browser creates a
        Semaphore identity~\cite{semaphore}, keeps its secret on the device, and
        offers a backup file that restores it on another device. Only the
        identity's public commitment goes to the registrar, which publishes the
        list of commitments in the same way. To vote, the browser downloads the
        list, checks it against the root recorded on-chain, and proves in zero
        knowledge that it holds the secret behind one of the commitments,
        without saying which. The proof also binds the choice and a nullifier,
        a value that is the same every time a given voter votes in a given
        election, so the contract rejects a second ballot without learning who
        sent either. A relayer submits the ballot and pays its gas, so a voter
        needs no wallet and no cryptocurrency. Any wallet can submit the ballot
        instead if the relayer is unavailable.
\end{itemize}

Around the contracts sit three off-chain services. The registrar checks a
voter's institute email address, through Google sign-in limited to the
institute's domain, and returns a signed receipt for every registration. The relayer is a separate process that checks
each ballot completely before paying for it and keeps no request logs. The
indexer is a read cache that makes election lists and ballot tables quick to
show; nothing depends on it for correctness. Once an election's voter list is
published, its page offers an audit that recounts the result in the browser
from the chain and IPFS alone, and in an anonymous election re-verifies every
ballot's proof. The contracts are
deployed on Sepolia and their source is verified on Sourcify~\cite{sourcify}, so
anyone can check the deployed bytecode against the code described here.

\paragraph{Headline results.}
Under Ethereum mainnet's current rules, measured locally, a tier 2 ballot costs
80,221 gas and a tier 3 ballot 296,692 gas: anonymity costs about 216,000 gas
per ballot. The tier 3 ballot's cost does not depend on the size of the voter
list: from 4 to 65,536 voters it stays between 296,656 and 296,692 gas, and
publishing the list costs the same at every size. Sepolia, however, now runs
the Glamsterdam upgrade~\cite{glamsterdam}, whose EIP-8037~\cite{eip8037}
charges 1,530 gas for every byte of new state. Creating state there costs
several times more: deploying the tier 2 election contract took 14.66 million
gas against 2.08 million under mainnet's rules, and creating an election about
3.09 million against about 0.63 million. Section~6 therefore reports gas under
both rule sets.

The work that does grow with the list moves to the voter's device. Proving a
ballot takes 0.57\,s at two voters and 1.52\,s at 65,536 on one ARM64 CPU
core, while rebuilding the list to check it grows to 15.8\,s at 65,536. On
Sepolia, with voters casting one at a time, a ballot was counted a median of
10.7\,s after the voter pressed Cast (16 ballots; slowest 18.4\,s). Under
load, 64 voters casting at the same moment were all counted in the next block.
In a simulated usability test with ten participants played by language models,
all ten registered and voted, two of them with help.
On an Android phone a ballot is proved in under two seconds for any list up
to 4,096 voters, and in 9.1\,s at 65,536.

The contracts pass all 92 Foundry tests, with 98.9\% of lines and every branch
covered, and Slither reports no findings once its timestamp
detector, which flags the deliberate time-gated phases, is set aside. On a
local chain, with the clock moved ahead between phases, a browser test runs a
complete anonymous approval election with three voters who have no wallets,
two of whom move to a new browser, one with the backup file and one with the
private link. On Sepolia, anonymous elections with up to ten voters have run
end to end in real time.

We chose voting from the available domains because it has the widest gap
between what an organiser claims and what anyone else can verify. A wrong stock
count in a warehouse is caught at the next physical count, but a wrong election
result is rarely checked again. In an election the participants are opponents,
so the record has to be one that none of them controls and that anyone can
check. A public blockchain provides such a record: the rules are enforced by
the network, and anyone can re-run them.

The design does not solve every problem in electronic voting. Its limits,
namely the correctness of the voter list, coercion, the trusted setup, and
privacy from the relayer, are set out together in Section~\ref{sec:trust}.

The rest of this report is organised as follows. Section~2 revisits the
problem and existing systems in the light of what was built. Section~3 restates
the requirements and the trust model and records what changed during
implementation. Section~4 describes the deployed architecture, Section~5 the
contracts, and Section~6 the measurements against the evaluation parameters of
the Evaluation~1 report. Section~7 compares the plan as executed with the plan
as proposed, and Section~8 concludes.
