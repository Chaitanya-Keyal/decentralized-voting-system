\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

An election result is acted on straight away and never checked again, so a
wrong tally is never exposed later. Auditing the operator does not fix this; it
only moves the trust to the auditor, who relies on the operator's own records.

\projectname{} puts the rules that decide an election into contract code on a
public test blockchain: who may vote, when each phase changes, and which ballots
count. Anyone who doubts the result can recount it from the chain. The nullifier
that blocks a second ballot never reveals whose ballot it was.

Only the root, the voter count and the list's IPFS address go on-chain, and the
booth builds its proof from the published list. This avoids Semaphore's on-chain
groups, which would cost a transaction per voter. The constant cost is per
operation, not per election: total gas still grows with the number of ballots,
and the work that grows with the roll moves to the registrar, the auditors and
the booth.

The limits in Section~\ref{sec:trust} remain. Eligibility depends on a trusted
setup we did not run, one registrar decides who is on the roll, a voter can be
forced to hand over their secret, and secrecy from our operator holds only while
the relayer runs separately from the registrar. So \projectname{} takes the
operator out of the ballot box and the count, but one party still decides who
may vote.

Nothing has been measured yet. Evaluation~2 will replace these targets with
numbers. C1 measures gas from ten voters to a hundred thousand: tier 3 should be
flat and cost more than tier 2 throughout. C2 checks whether a large roll can be
handled on a phone. D4 needs no graph: with our backend switched off during the
demo, the audit dashboard must still read the chain and recount the tally.
Missed targets will be reported too.
