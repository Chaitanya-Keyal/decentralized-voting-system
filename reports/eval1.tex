\subsection{The centralized status quo}

\stub{Describe how elections are administered today (paper ballots, EVMs,
  centrally hosted e-voting). Be concrete and fair: state what these systems do

\subsection{Trust concentrated in a single operator}

A conventional electronic voting system asks the electorate to trust one operator
with four separable responsibilities at once: the voter roll, the ballot box, the
count, and the audit log. Table~\ref{tab:trust} states each assumption and how a
blockchain-based design removes the need to make it.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Trust assumptions in a centralized system, and how \projectname{} removes each.}
  \label{tab:trust}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{6.0cm}L{9.7cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Assumption the voter must make} & \textbf{How \projectname{} removes it}                                                                      \\
    \midrule
    The voter roll is correct
                                            & The roll is committed as a Merkle root on-chain before voting opens and cannot be changed afterwards.       \\
    Nobody voted twice
                                            & Each ballot carries a zero-knowledge nullifier; the contract rejects duplicates without learning who voted. \\
    My ballot was counted
                                            & Every ballot is an on-chain transaction the voter can locate for themselves.                                \\
    The count is honest
                                            & Tallies are incremented by contract code, not by an operator.                                               \\
    The audit log was not edited
                                            & The log is the chain.                                                                                       \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\stub{Expand each row into prose. For each one, name a concrete failure mode of
  the centralized system: what exactly can a dishonest or compromised operator
  do, and why can a voter not detect it?}

\subsection{Why paper is not the answer either}

\stub{Paper ballots remove some of these problems and introduce others: cost,
  delay, and the fact that a voter cannot verify their own ballot once it enters
  the box. Establish that the problem is real in both directions.}

\subsection{Why a blockchain is the right tool here}

\stub{Argue this honestly. A blockchain is the right tool when multiple mutually
  distrusting parties need a shared, append-only record that no single one of them
  controls. Say where this is genuinely true for voting, and be explicit about
  what a blockchain does \emph{not} fix: voter eligibility still depends on an
  off-chain identity check, which remains a trusted step.}

\subsection{Actors and stakeholders}

Table~\ref{tab:actors} lists each actor, what the system permits them to do, and
what it structurally prevents them from doing. The final column is the important
one: a permission model is only meaningful when it is stated in terms of what a
privileged party \emph{cannot} do.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Actors, their capabilities, and their structural limits.}
  \label{tab:actors}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{2.8cm}L{6.0cm}L{6.4cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Actor} & \textbf{Can do}                                                                                  & \textbf{Cannot do} \\
    \midrule
    Election Authority
                   & Create an election, set candidates and time windows, publish metadata, trigger finalization
                   & Cast or alter a ballot; change the voter root once registration closes; stop the count                                \\
    Registrar
                   & Verify eligibility off-chain, collect identity commitments, publish the roll's root and leaf set
                   & See how anyone voted; add voters after the root is frozen                                                             \\
    Voter
                   & Register an identity commitment, cast exactly one ballot, verify that their ballot landed
                   & Vote twice; vote without being in the committed set                                                                   \\
    Candidate
                   & Be listed on a ballot, publish a manifesto
                   & Anything privileged                                                                                                   \\
    Auditor / Observer
                   & Re-derive the full tally from chain data, verify every proof, check the roll size
                   & Alter anything                                                                                                        \\
    Relayer
                   & Pay gas on a voter's behalf
                   & Forge, modify, or replay a ballot                                                                                     \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\stub{For each actor, write a short paragraph justifying \emph{why} they need
  the capabilities listed and why the restrictions are the right ones. This is the
  part the rubric asks to be justified, so do not just restate the table.}

\subsection{Smart contract design}

\stub{Describe each contract and its responsibility: the election lifecycle state
  machine, the factory that deploys elections as minimal proxy clones, the voter
  registry that stores roots, the tally library, and the ERC-2771 forwarder that
  lets voters transact without holding gas.}

  Registration, Voting, Tallying, Finalized, with Cancelled reachable only before
  voting opens. State which transitions are time-gated and which require an
  explicit call.}

\subsection{Anonymous eligibility and double-vote prevention}

\stub{Explain the mechanism in plain language before any notation. A voter holds
  a secret; its commitment sits as a leaf in a Merkle tree whose root is on-chain.
  To vote, the voter proves in zero knowledge that they know a secret matching
  \emph{some} leaf, without revealing which. The proof carries a nullifier derived
  deterministically from the secret and the election identifier, so a second ballot
  from the same voter is rejected while the chain still learns nothing about who
  they are.}

\subsection{Scalability strategy}

The system is designed so that gas cost is constant in the size of the
electorate. This depends on one decision: the membership tree is built off-chain
and only its root is written on-chain.

\stub{Explain the consequence: a roll of ten voters and a roll of a hundred
  thousand both cost a single 32-byte storage write. State the tradeoff too: the
  tree is not reconstructible from chain data alone, so the registrar must publish
  the leaf set for auditors, and that publication is what keeps the roll auditable.}
